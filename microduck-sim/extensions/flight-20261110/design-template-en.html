Flying MicroDuck: four-fan jet pack design

MicroDuck is our small two-legged duck robot (about 27 cm tall). This pack adds four 50 mm electric ducted fans (EDFs: small fans inside a tube), two per side in two pods called nacelles, two 4S lithium batteries (four cells in series, about 14.8 V), and a flight controller with a downward-looking optical-flow sensor on its back. The goal is to fly up onto a table about 0.75 m high and back down, while keeping the duck's walking, turning, bending to pick things up, and head motion. 中文版

Status ({{state_verified}}): the printed parts are modelled and the print files are released (revision {{release}}), not printed yet. Electrical

The pack has its own power and shares only three signal wires with the duck (ground, transmit, receive); the duck's servos and its A7Z computer stay on the duck's own 2-cell battery.

Power

Each battery plugs into an XT60 pigtail (12 AWG); both pigtails and the capacitor are soldered to the ESC's two battery tabs, where the batteries join in parallel.

Each of the ESC's four motor pad sets gets three 16 AWG wires ending in 3.5 mm female bullets, which take the fans' own male bullets, so each fan unplugs on its own.

Flight controller

The FC sits on the ESC; one 8-pin lead carries power, throttle signals and the current reading.

Serial port 4: the flow sensor. Serial port 1: the A7Z.

Link to the duck

A 20 cm SH1.0-to-Dupont lead from FC serial port 1 to the A7Z header: pin 14 ground, pin 16 A7Z transmit to FC receive, pin 18 FC transmit to A7Z receive. The lead's 5 V wire is cut and insulated; the two supplies share only ground.

This A7Z serial port (UART4, pins 16 and 18) has not been enabled or tested; it must be switched on in the operating system first.

Soldering

12 motor pads, 12 bullets, 2 battery tabs (three wires each), 4 XT60 cups. All on the bench before anything goes on the duck.

Charging

The batteries are strapped to the trays: unplug the XT60, open the strap and lift the battery out to charge; the pack stays on.

Control

The flight controller holds attitude and height by itself, and the A7Z only gives it tasks; the walking policy takes no part in the air.

  • The FC holds attitude. It has its own gyroscope and adjusts the four fans hundreds of times a second to keep the body level.
  • The flow sensor holds height and drift. The rangefinder gives height and the optical flow gives sideways speed, both straight into the FC.
  • The A7Z runs the mission. Over MAVLink (the standard drone telemetry protocol) it tells the FC how high to climb, where to move and when to land, and switches the legs' control mode at take-off and touchdown.

  • Roll: left pair against right pair, {{fan_y}} mm lever arm: strong.
  • Pitch: front pair against rear pair, only {{fan_pitch}} mm apart: weaker.
  • Yaw: the ducts' stator vanes cancel the swirl, so reaction torque is weak. Hence the {{cant}} degree tilt: speeding up fans 1 and 2 and slowing 3 and 4 makes the jets' sideways components turn the body.

Software

None of the flight software is written yet; below is what has to be written and the leg hand-over already proven in simulation.

Flight program on the A7Z

Talks MAVLink to the FC over serial: heartbeat, take off, move, land, emergency stop; reads back height, attitude, voltage, current. It does not touch the servos.

What the legs do in the air

Proven in simulation: when the estimated foot height passes 3 cm in the climb, the legs switch to a locked standing pose; when it drops below 3 cm in the descent, the walking policy takes over to catch the landing. Running the walking policy in the air makes the legs flail and is not usable. sim

This needs the FC to send its range height to the A7Z in real time; that link does not exist yet.

Detecting the pack

Shawn's requirement: the A7Z detects whether the pack is fitted and picks jetpack mode by itself, with no manual switch. The plan is the FC's once-a-second heartbeat.

Gap: the FC is powered only by the flight batteries, so a fitted pack with its batteries unplugged sends no heartbeat and looks like no pack. Either a detection that works unpowered, or the page shows the detected mode with a manual override.

Flight panel in the phone app

Added to the existing remote page: arm, take off to a height, nudge, land, stop; shows height, voltage, current and FC state.

The stop button goes phone → Wi-Fi → Mac mini → A7Z → FC, and any stuck link blocks it. The backstops are the FC's own {{p_FS_GCS_TIMEOUT}} s link-loss landing and a tether during tests.

Effect on existing behaviours

Geometrically, standing, walking, bending and head motion all clear the pack. Dynamically, walking with the pack works in simulation but toes out, and the bend still falls; training is running. Once fitted the pack stays on (Shawn's requirement), so all of this must hold with the pack.

One flight, step by step

This sequence runs in simulation (three hovers, three flights up, three down, all succeed at rated thrust); it has not run on the robot.

  1. Prepare. The duck stands on its walking policy. The A7Z checks the FC heartbeat, voltage and position estimate.
  2. Arm and take off. The fans go to take-off thrust; at 3 cm foot height the legs lock in the standing pose. The legs are still moving at lift-off, so the robot drifts sideways during take-off, up to {{sim_drift}} m in simulation.
  3. Fly over the table. The flow sensor is at the back of the body; when its reading suddenly drops by about 0.75 m, the whole duck is over the table and the FC re-bases its height on the table top.
  4. Descend and land. Below 3 cm foot height the walking policy takes the legs back; touchdown is at most {{sim_vz}} m/s, then the fans stop, the FC disarms and the duck keeps standing.

No known recipeIn simulation the stand-in flight controller re-bases height at the table edge by design. Real ArduPilot holding height on the rangefinder will see the "ground" jump 0.75 m up, think it is falling and add throttle; the right settings for a smooth table crossing must be found in tethered tests.

A power loss at 1 m breaks printed parts, so early tests are all tethered, with safety glasses.

Tests and pass conditions

  1. Fans arrive: check they fall inside the nacelle's size band and see which side the leads leave.
  2. Print both plates and assemble following the assembly guide: FLIGHT-PACK-BUILD.html (Chinese).
  3. M3 pull-out test: print a 20 × 20 × 12 mm block on the plate settings with a 3.1 mm sideways hole, drive an M3 × 12 and hang weights. If it holds about 186 N (about 19 kg), the nacelle lugs keep a safety factor of 2 when the robot is lifted by one nacelle.
  4. Spin check: run one fan at a time and check spin and airflow direction (last steps of the assembly guide).
  5. FC setup: set the parameters above; check BATT_AMP_PERVLT against a clamp meter or the charger's refill.
  6. First tethered hover: read the logged hover current and feel the XT60 plugs after 60 s. This is also the thrust measurement: at the low end of the current range plugs and endurance are fine; at the high end, solder the battery leads directly or fit bigger plugs. Write the logged hover throttle into MOT_THST_HOVER.
